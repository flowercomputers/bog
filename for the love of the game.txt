If I had to guess where it all started, I reckon it would be here. For years, I had been one of those eleuthromaniacal freaks running a Arch Linux with a tiling window manager on a nearly 10-year-old laptop. And I loved it. But, I was enrolling in university, and needed to either put a webcam on my dinosaur of a Windows desktop (which I used almost exclusively for Ableton Live and Premiere Pro) or just buy an Apple laptop. In the interest of portability, it was out with the ancient Thinkbook and in with the M3 Macbook Pro. I was pretty butthurt about the switch, but I was really looking forward to school; I didn't go to college at the appropriate time, and now, in my early twenties, this was my chance to rectify that incredibly poor life decision.

My M3 Macbook Pro, which I am writing this on, has been very good to me. It makes everything quite easy, really! However, ease-of-use was never what I liked about using a computer. What I am realizing now that I liked was _making the computer easy to use_. Arch Linux is a notable example of this; it gives you very little to start with. Out of the box, it's an environment that's outright hostile to anyone expecting the modern experience of using a computer! Just some utilities and a package manager, given to the user over a TTY. From this, you shape the system toward whatever it is that you wanted. Want that "modern" computer experience? Install and configure Gnome, or KDE, or whatever! My point here, though, is that Arch Linux can be pretty difficult to use itself, but it gives the user the ability to consider what "ease of use" might mean for themselves. For me, it was always a lightly configured tiling window manager (in later years, `sway`), plus tools like `micro`. Of course, naming these tools here opens me up to criticism—("why not vim?", "why not emacs?", "why not awesome", etc.), but herein lies my point—those might be good for you, but I found sway, micro, and the like just made my life easier!

The experience of endlessly configuring and reconfiguring my environment shaped my thinking about what "ease of use" is and what end it might serve. So much so that when I went back to school, I decided to study ergonomics such that I might someday be able to approach "ease of use" as far as programming environments from an academic perspective to figure out the best way to make new tools for all of us.

> Dating a tech bro is all fun and games until you get optimized out of their life.

There's this itch in technically minded people to optimize things—to take things in their natural state of chaos and mold them (usually) to some useful end. This reaches far beyond the technical realms, though, and into our lives as a whole. Maybe the most naked example of this has been the success of the "longevity" industry—developing "protocols" based on "data driven insights" for everything from "what to eat and when" to "what experimental gene therapies to subject yourself to and what country to buy them from." This is obviously this itch turned toward optimizing their current habits toward the end of life extension. And, for the most part, I'm sympathetic to people who engage with this; as much as Bryan Johnson tries to spin what he's doing as some kind of project for the good of humanity (or his enemies to spin it as brazen capitalism), it will always be obvious that it's just something that he enjoys doing. It all represents some kind of "love of the game." For technical people, the game they love just happens to be optimization.
